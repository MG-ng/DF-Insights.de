Trading the German Electricity Price

A practical guide for a private person in Germany: no company, no power plant, and no electricity contract of your own (for example a tenant whose power is part of the rent).

Not financial, tax or legal advice. This page explains how the market works and which products exist. It does not recommend buying or selling anything. Leveraged products can lose their whole value within hours. Rules and products change; check the linked primary sources and your broker's documents before you act. Last reviewed: November 2026.

1) The short version

  • You cannot buy electricity itself as an investment, and there is no plain, unleveraged ETF or ETC on the German power price.
  • The most realistic route for a private investor is a leveraged certificate (Mini Future or Turbo) on an EEX German Power future, bought through a normal German securities account (Depot). Vontobel, for example, issued long and short Mini Futures on the EEX German Power Base Quarter Future in 2023.
  • Direct EEX futures are built for utilities and traders: one contract is 1 MW for every hour of the delivery period, so a single year contract covers about 8,760 MWh. Very few retail brokers offer them.
  • Betting on the power price on Polymarket or similar sites is not an option from Germany: the Joint Gambling Authority (GGL) says such bets cannot be licensed and taking part can be a criminal offence.
  • Gains are taxed with the flat capital gains tax (Abgeltungsteuer, 25 % plus solidarity surcharge and possibly church tax). The old 20,000 € cap on offsetting losses from derivatives was abolished by the 2024 Annual Tax Act.

2) How the German power price is made

Before you trade it, it helps to know which "power price" you are actually trading. There are two layers:

Spot market (EPEX SPOT)

Every day at noon, the day-ahead auction sets one price for each quarter hour of the next day for the German-Luxembourg bidding zone. This is the price you see on the home page of this site, published by SMARD. It swings a lot: it can be negative on a sunny, windy Sunday and several hundred euros per MWh in a cold, windless evening, exactly the Dunkelflaute this site is about. Private persons cannot trade on EPEX SPOT; membership is for companies.

Futures market (EEX)

The EEX German Power Future (often still called "Phelix DE") is a financial contract on the average day-ahead price over a future delivery period: a day, weekend, week, month, quarter or year. EEX calls it the most liquid financial power future in Europe. Two load profiles are traded:

  • Base: every hour of the delivery period.
  • Peak: weekdays from 8:00 to 20:00.

The futures price is the market's expectation of the future average spot price. That matters: a Dunkelflaute next Tuesday moves the day and week contracts strongly, but it barely moves a contract for next year. Almost every retail product refers to a quarter or year future, so it reacts to the long-term story (gas and carbon prices, power plant capacity, expansion of renewables, policy) much more than to the weather of the coming days.

4) Exchange-traded certificates on the power price

Banks issue leverage certificates (Hebelprodukte) whose underlying is an EEX German Power future. They are bonds of the issuing bank, traded like a share on German exchanges such as Börse Stuttgart or Börse Frankfurt and through most online brokers. In 2023 Vontobel introduced long and short Mini Futures on the EEX German Power Base Quarter Future and called itself the first issuer to do so. HypoVereinsbank's onemarkets brand has also covered the power price in its certificate blog.

How to find them

  1. Open the product search of an issuer or an exchange and filter by underlying "Strom", "German Power" or "Phelix".
  2. Check the exact underlying: which contract (Base or Peak, quarter or year) and how the issuer rolls from one contract to the next. A roll can shift the price level of your certificate.
  3. Check leverage, knock-out level and financing costs. With a Mini Future, the strike is adjusted daily by a financing rate, which costs long holders money every day.
  4. Look at the bid-ask spread and the trading hours. If the underlying future does not trade, the issuer may widen spreads or stop quoting.

What you have to know

  • Knock-out means total loss. In August 2022 the German year contract went to nearly 1,000 €/MWh and then fell by more than half within weeks. Many leveraged positions would not have survived that on either side.
  • Issuer risk. A certificate is unsecured debt of the issuer. If the issuer fails, you can lose your money even if your view was right. There is no deposit insurance.
  • New BaFin rules for Turbos. Since 16 June 2026, BaFin's product intervention for turbo certificates applies to retail investors in Germany: you must pass a short knowledge test at your bank (to be repeated every six months), you see a standard warning that on average 7 of 10 retail clients lose money with turbo certificates, and bonuses for trading them are banned. BaFin found that about three out of four retail traders lost money with turbos.
  • A low-leverage long Mini Future (leverage close to 1) is the closest thing to a "power price tracker", and some use it as a hedge against rising household power costs. It only works as a hedge if your own bill actually follows the wholesale price, which is rarely true for a tenant whose power is in the rent.

5) EEX power futures

The futures themselves are traded on EEX in Leipzig and cleared by its clearing house ECC, which takes over the counterparty risk. Key facts from EEX:

  • Delivery rate: 1 MW for each hour of the delivery period. One year contract (base) is about 8,760 MWh; one quarter contract about 2,160 MWh; one month about 720 MWh.
  • Tick size: 0.01 €/MWh.
  • Trading hours: 8:00 to 18:00 (CET/CEST) on exchange days.
  • Maturities: the next days and weekends, weeks, up to 24 months, 11 quarters and 10 years ahead.
  • Settlement: financially against the day-ahead price (cash settlement).

What this means for you: at a price of, say, 90 €/MWh a single year contract has a face value of roughly 790,000 €, and every move of 1 €/MWh changes your profit or loss by about 8,760 €. A month contract is about 12 times smaller, but still moves by about 720 € per 1 €/MWh. Trading needs a broker that is a clearing member at ECC (or uses one), and most German retail brokers do not offer EEX power at all. Ask your broker directly before you open an account for this purpose.

Futures can cost more than your deposit: if prices move against you, the broker demands more margin (Nachschuss). BaFin consulted in 2022 on banning futures with margin calls for retail clients; whatever your broker offers, read whether losses can exceed your account balance. Anyone who meets the conditions to be treated as a "professional client" under MiFID II gives up these retail protections.

6) CFDs

A CFD (contract for difference) pays the price change of an underlying without owning it. If a CFD provider lists German power at all, EU and BaFin rules for retail clients apply:

  • Maximum leverage 10:1 for commodities other than gold.
  • Positions are closed automatically when your margin falls below half of the initial margin.
  • Negative balance protection: you cannot lose more than the money in your CFD account. CFDs with an obligation to pay more are banned in Germany since 2017.
  • Providers must state how many of their retail clients lose money. In practice this is usually well over half.

Power CFDs are rare. Check that the provider is authorised in the EU (BaFin company database or the regulator of its home country) and what exactly the CFD refers to.

7) Indirect exposure: utilities, carbon, gas

If you want exposure to the forces behind the power price rather than the price itself, ordinary, unleveraged products are available in any Depot:

  • Utility shares: generators with a lot of merchant (unhedged) output profit from high wholesale prices. Grid operators earn regulated returns and react much less. Utility ETFs (for example on the MSCI World Utilities index) are dominated by US companies and follow stock markets more than power prices.
  • Carbon (EU emission allowances, EUA): the carbon price is part of the cost of every coal and gas power plant and therefore of the German power price. ETCs exist, for example WisdomTree Carbon (ISIN JE00BP2PWW32), which tracks ICE EUA futures through a swap.
  • Natural gas: gas plants set the German power price in many hours. Gas ETCs exist, but check whether they track European (TTF) or US (Henry Hub) gas; the US price has little to do with German power.

All of these are futures-based or equity products with their own risks (roll costs, issuer risk for ETCs, company risk for shares).

8) Betting and prediction markets

Betting on "Will the day-ahead price exceed 300 €/MWh next week?" sounds attractive, but from Germany it is not a legal option:

  • Under the State Treaty on Gambling (GlüStV 2021), only bets on sports and horse races can be licensed. Bets on political, social, economic or other events ("Gesellschaftswetten") cannot.
  • The GGL warned in September 2025 that bets such as those on Polymarket are "nicht erlaubnisfähig und damit illegal", and that taking part is punishable. In August 2026 it repeated that participation from Germany is not allowed and can be a criminal offence (§ 285 StGB).
  • Polymarket itself lists Germany as a blocked country since August 2026, and using a VPN breaks its terms. Winnings from an unlicensed provider are not legally enforceable.

If you want to test your forecasting skill without money at stake, play-money prediction markets are a legal alternative. Or simply write down your forecast for next week's prices and compare it with the SMARD data on this site.

9) The physical route: tariffs and balcony solar

The most direct way for a household to "trade" the spot price is to buy power at it:

  • Dynamic tariffs: since 1 January 2025 every supplier in Germany must offer a dynamic tariff to customers with a smart meter (intelligentes Messsystem), § 41a EnWG. The energy price then follows the exchange price and can change every quarter hour. You only gain if you can shift consumption (an electric car, a heat pump, a battery) into cheap hours; for a small flat with about 2,000 kWh a year the Verbraucherzentrale NRW says it is usually not worth it.
  • As a tenant without your own contract, this route is closed unless your flat has its own meter and you sign your own supply contract. If your power is part of an all-inclusive rent, your landlord carries the price risk, so you have little to hedge in the first place.
  • Balcony solar (Steckersolargerät): up to 800 W can be registered in a simplified process, and since October 2024 tenants have a legal claim to their landlord's permission in most cases. It lowers what you buy rather than earning trading profit, and it needs your own meter to pay off.

10) Taxes in Germany

  • Flat tax: gains from certificates, futures, CFDs, ETCs and shares are income from capital (§ 20 EStG) and taxed at 25 % Abgeltungsteuer plus 5.5 % solidarity surcharge on that tax and, if you are a church member, church tax.
  • Allowance: the first 1,000 € (2,000 € for married couples filing jointly) of capital income per year are tax-free. Give your German bank a Freistellungsauftrag.
  • Losses from derivatives: from 2021 losses from futures, options and CFDs ("Termingeschäfte") could only be offset up to 20,000 € a year. The Annual Tax Act 2024 (Jahressteuergesetz 2024) abolished this cap for all open cases, so these losses can again be offset in full against capital gains.
  • Foreign brokers usually do not withhold German tax. You must declare the gains yourself in Anlage KAP of your tax return.
  • Gambling winnings are generally not taxed in Germany, but that does not make an unlicensed betting site legal (see section 8).

Ask a tax adviser (Steuerberater) or a Lohnsteuerhilfeverein for your own case.

11) Using Dunkelflaute data for a trading view

This site's data can help you understand why the price moves. Some things to look at before forming a view:

  • Seasonality. Long Dunkelflauten happen almost only between November and February. Use the Dunkelflaute search to see how often and how long they were in past winters. A cold, windless first quarter is priced into the Q1 future long before January.
  • Residual load drives the price. Residual load is demand minus wind and solar. When it is high, gas and coal plants set the price, so the price tracks gas and carbon prices. The time-series chart on the home page shows residual load next to the day-ahead price.
  • Weather forecasts move the short end. The forecast page uses Open-Meteo forecasts for the coming days. Those move day and week contracts, which retail products rarely track. A forecast of a Dunkelflaute next week is not a reason to buy a certificate on next year's contract.
  • Others see the same data. Weather forecasts are public, and professional traders use better models and faster data. Any edge for a private person is small at best.

12) Risk checklist

Before you place a trade, you should be able to answer each of these:

  1. Which contract exactly does my product track (Base or Peak, month, quarter or year)?
  2. How and when does it roll, and what does that cost?
  3. At what price is it knocked out, and how far away is that in percent?
  4. Can I lose more than my stake? (Futures: yes. Certificates and EU CFDs: no.)
  5. Who is the issuer or counterparty, and what happens if they fail?
  6. What does the spread cost me on the way in and out?
  7. Is this money I can afford to lose completely?
